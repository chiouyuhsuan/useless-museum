field notes · Mood World · exhibit no.01

I built a place for strangers to rate their mood. I expected the internet. I got kindness.

Mood World asks one small thing: how do you feel today, 1 to 7? Then it shows you your country's mood against the world's. I built it expecting the usual anonymous internet. What 247 strangers actually left behind surprised me.

By The Curator · Field notes on a small experiment in feelings · ~6 min read · Last updated 30 July 2026

In short

Mood World is a 10-second daily mood check-in: rate today 1–7, and see how your country feels versus the rest of the world. I added anonymous notes so people had a reason to return — and braced for the internet, where research finds negativity tends to outcompete kindness. Instead, across 247 check-ins over ~30 days, almost no one left anything unkind — even people rating their day a 3 or 4 out of 7. The most-liked notes were all small acts of encouragement to strangers. The data quietly argued the opposite of what I expected about people online.

Mood World asks for almost nothing. Ten seconds, maybe less: how do you feel today, on a scale of 1 to 7? Tap a face, and you're in. In return, it shows you something oddly comforting — how your country is feeling today, and how that compares to everyone else on the map. A tiny, living readout of the planet's collective mood.

The idea came from a simple frustration. I wanted to make something people could use every day without it feeling like a burden. Social media and short video have already taken most of our attention; adding one more demanding thing felt hopeless. So Mood World had to be the opposite of demanding — one tap, no account required, no feed to scroll. Just: here's how the world feels, and here's your one small mark on it.

01Why a mood map might actually mean something

On its own, any single person's mood is noise. Who cares that a stranger rated today a 5? But I kept thinking about what happens when the sample gets large enough. That's when the interesting questions appear.

It was World Cup season when I was building it, and I couldn't stop wondering: does a nation's team winning or losing — once every four years — actually move the mood of its people? Anyone who's watched the news knows how emotional football fans get. Or take disasters: a typhoon sweeping across Asia, a market crash in Seoul. With enough check-ins, could a tiny mood app actually detect the emotional shadow of real-world events? That was the dream — turning millions of individually-meaningless moods into a signal about how the world is actually doing.

My modest goal was 100 daily check-ins across the English-speaking world. Enough to see a pattern. Not viral — just alive.

02The problem: a number nobody comes back for

Here's the flaw I saw early. Looking at a trend line and other people's average scores is mildly interesting exactly once. There's no reason to return. A dashboard doesn't miss you.

So I added a small human layer: after you check in, you can leave a note for the other people checking in that day. And if someone finds your note helpful, they can like it — so the app quietly tracks whose notes are the most encouraging, the most loved.

It's not a novel feature. Anyone could think of it. But what interested me wasn't the mechanic — it was the data it would quietly accumulate. Because I had a hypothesis about it, and I was fairly sure I knew how it would turn out.

03What I expected — and what 247 strangers actually did

Here was my quiet, cynical prediction. Give anonymous strangers a text box, especially ones having a bad day, and you get the internet: the terse complaints, the unhelpful venting, the small casual unkindness that anonymity is supposed to unlock. The research broadly agrees the internet leans this way — platform algorithms reward outrage, and negative content tends to outcompete the positive.

Across roughly 30 days, Mood World collected 247 check-ins. Not many. But enough to notice something I didn't expect: almost no one left anything unkind. Not even the people rating their day a 3 or a 4 out of 7 — the ones my cynical model predicted would be the source of the venting. They just... weren't.

And the notes that rose to the top — the most-liked ones, by actual strangers voting — were these:

"Today is one page. There are a lot more left to write."
"Sending a small bit of good energy your way, from someone you'll never meet."
"Someone, somewhere, is glad you're here today."

That's it. That's what people left for each other, unprompted, anonymously, for nothing. No one was watching. No one was being rewarded beyond a small sun-shaped like. And in a space I had genuinely expected to fill with low-grade negativity, near-strangers kept quietly reaching for the kindest thing they could think to say to someone they'd never meet.

The sample was small, and I won't overclaim it. But 247 people, given a private moment and a text box, chose to be gentle. That is not the internet I was bracing for.

I later tried to give people a reason to keep coming back — check in seven days and I'd donate a dollar to a related charity. It didn't move the numbers much. But I mention it because I did genuinely try; the emptiness of a project isn't always for lack of effort.

04Why 247 was, quietly, enough

Let me be honest about the number. My first reaction to "247 check-ins, all time" is disappointment. I wanted the 100-a-day map that could feel a nation's heartbeat. I got a quiet room instead.

But there's a person I think about. Long after I mostly stopped watching the dashboard, one user kept showing up — every single day, checking in from Switzerland. I don't even know if it's true; users pick their own country, and I'll never verify it. But there he is, day after day, marking how he feels on a map almost no one looks at.

And here's the part that gets me: because of him, I still open it sometimes and check in myself — otherwise even I, the person who built it, would have drifted away. A stranger from Switzerland is the reason its maker still visits his own creation.

So was 247 a failure? On the spreadsheet, maybe. But 247 is also the number of times a real person paused, somewhere in the world, to say this is how I feel today — and, more often than not, to leave a little warmth for whoever came next. Mood World never became a barometer for the planet. It became something smaller and stranger and, I think, better: proof that when you give people a gentle, unwatched place to be honest, most of them are kind. And that sometimes one faithful stranger in Switzerland is enough to keep a small light on.
